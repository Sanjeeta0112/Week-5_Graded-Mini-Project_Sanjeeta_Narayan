One-Page Reflection: Prompting Strategy Findings and Recommendations

The evaluation of four prompting strategies, Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Few-Shot, Structured, and Zero-Shot, shows that all approaches performed relatively consistently in terms of accuracy and reliability, but meaningful differences emerged in quality and latency. The results demonstrate that prompt design affects not only response accuracy, but also execution speed and overall judged quality.

The Few-Shot strategy achieved the highest mean accuracy of 3.0, slightly outperforming CoT and Structured prompting at 2.9 and Zero-Shot at 2.8. This suggests that providing examples helps the model better understand the expected task, response pattern, or decision criteria. However, Few-Shot did not receive the highest judge score, scoring 19.1 compared with 19.6 for CoT. CoT therefore produced the strongest overall judge-assessed performance despite its slightly lower accuracy. This highlights an important distinction: the strategy that maximizes accuracy on a particular measure may not necessarily produce the strongest overall response quality.

Another positive finding is that all four strategies achieved a parse rate of 1.0 (100%). This indicates that every strategy consistently produced outputs that could be successfully parsed. From an application-development perspective, this is particularly important because a response that cannot be parsed can cause downstream processing failures even when its underlying content is correct. Since parsing performance was identical across the strategies, parse reliability does not provide a reason to prefer one approach over another in this experiment.

Latency reveals a clearer difference. Structured prompting was the fastest strategy, with a p50 latency of 1.120 seconds, followed by Few-Shot at 1.293 seconds. CoT and Zero-Shot were slower at 1.410 and 1.418 seconds respectively. Structured prompting therefore provides an attractive balance: its accuracy of 2.9 matches CoT, its judge score of 19.3 is competitive, and it delivers the lowest latency. Interestingly, Zero-Shot was both the least accurate and the slowest strategy in this dataset, suggesting that a simpler prompt does not automatically translate into faster or better performance.

The reported total cost is $0.0 for all strategies, so this experiment does not establish a meaningful cost difference among them. This should not be interpreted as proof that the strategies have identical production costs. A more useful future evaluation would record input tokens, output tokens, and actual model/API charges, particularly because Few-Shot and CoT prompts can require more tokens than simpler approaches.

Recommendations

Based on these findings, Structured prompting should be considered the best default strategy when balancing accuracy, quality, reliability, and latency. Few-Shot prompting should be preferred when maximizing the measured accuracy is the primary goal, particularly for tasks where representative examples can clearly demonstrate the expected output. CoT may be appropriate when judge-assessed reasoning quality is more important than response speed, as it achieved the highest judge score but had relatively high latency. Zero-Shot should remain useful as a simple baseline, but the current results do not provide a strong reason to select it over the other approaches.

Finally, these conclusions should be validated with a larger evaluation dataset and repeated runs before choosing a production strategy. Future experiments should compare statistical variation in accuracy and latency, token consumption, actual API cost, and failure rates across different task types. Overall, the experiment demonstrates that prompt engineering should be evaluated as a multi-objective optimization problem rather than solely on accuracy. In these results, Structured prompting offers the strongest practical trade-off, while Few-Shot and CoT remain valuable when accuracy or judged response quality respectively takes priority.
